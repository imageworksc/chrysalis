What Is Children’s Mental Health Therapy?

Children’s mental health therapy is professional support designed to help kids and teens understand emotions, manage anxiety, improve behavior, process trauma or grief, strengthen communication, and develop healthier coping skills. Depending on the child’s age and needs, treatment may include child counseling, play therapy, child-centered play therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, family involvement, and other individualized approaches.

When Should a Child See a Mental Health Therapist?

All children experience fear, sadness, frustration, and difficult behavior at times. Professional support may be appropriate when those concerns continue, become more severe, cause significant distress, or interfere with daily life.

These signs do not automatically mean your child has a mental health disorder. An assessment can help identify what may be contributing to the problem and determine which type of support may fit.

Signs that a child may benefit from a mental health assessment include:

  • Frequent anxiety, fear, panic, or excessive worry
  • Persistent sadness, withdrawal, hopelessness, or irritability
  • Anger, aggression, defiance, or repeated emotional outbursts
  • Difficulty calming down or managing strong emotions
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, energy, or concentration
  • School avoidance or declining academic performance
  • Bullying, friendship difficulties, or social isolation
  • Trauma, grief, or another significant loss
  • Difficulty adjusting to divorce, relocation, illness, or family changes
  • Behavioral or emotional concerns related to ADHD
  • Loss of interest in activities the child once enjoyed
  • Statements about self-harm, suicide, or hurting someone else

If you recognize your child in this list, you do not have to figure out the next step alone.

Mental Health Concerns Therapy Can Address

Therapy for Childhood Anxiety and Excessive Worry

Anxiety can cause children to avoid school, social situations, sleeping alone, separating from a caregiver, or participating in everyday activities. Therapy helps children recognize anxious thoughts, understand physical reactions, and develop healthier ways to manage fear and worry.

Therapy for Childhood Depression and Withdrawal

Depression in children can appear as sadness, irritability, low energy, isolation, difficulty concentrating, or loss of interest. Therapy helps children communicate what they are feeling, rebuild healthy routines, and reconnect with family, friends, and activities.

Therapy for Anger and Behavioral Concerns

Frequent outbursts, aggression, defiance, or impulsive behavior may be connected to emotional distress, communication difficulties, environmental stress, or an underlying behavioral health condition. Therapy helps children identify triggers, manage strong reactions, and develop healthier responses.

Therapy for Childhood Trauma

Trauma-informed therapy helps children process frightening or overwhelming experiences, understand their reactions, rebuild a sense of safety, and develop coping strategies—without forcing them to discuss experiences before they are ready.

Therapy for Grief and Significant Loss

Children often struggle to understand or express grief following a death, separation, relocation, illness, or another major loss. Therapy provides an age-appropriate setting to explore feelings, ask questions, and adjust to change.

Therapy for School and Social Difficulties

Mental health concerns can affect attendance, concentration, academic performance, classroom behavior, friendships, and confidence. Therapy helps children strengthen communication, emotional regulation, coping, and problem-solving skills.

Therapy for Family Changes and Conflict

Divorce, separation, remarriage, relocation, illness, or conflict at home can affect a child’s emotional security. Individual and family therapy can help children and caregivers communicate more effectively and adjust to changes within the family.

Mental Health Therapy for Children and Teens

Treatment begins with an assessment to understand what your child is experiencing, how long the concerns have been present, and how they affect life at home, at school, and in relationships.

It will be based on your child’s developmental level, communication style, symptoms, strengths, and goals—not a one-size-fits-all program. Therapy may use conversation, structured activities, games, drawing, coping exercises, caregiver participation, or a combination of approaches.

Depending on the assessment and treatment plan, therapy may include:

  • Individual mental health therapy
  • Child counseling
  • Play therapy and play-based techniques
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy
  • Behavior-focused therapy
  • Trauma-informed therapy
  • Family therapy
  • Parent and caregiver guidance
  • Psychiatric evaluation and medication management when appropriate

Play Therapy for Children

What Is Play Therapy?

Play therapy uses play and developmentally appropriate activities to help children communicate, express emotions, process experiences, and develop healthier coping and problem-solving skills.

Children—especially younger children—may not be able to explain difficult feelings through conversation alone. Play gives them another way to communicate what they are feeling, remembering, fearing, or struggling to understand.

Play therapy and play-based techniques may include:

  • Drawing and creative activities
  • Toys, dolls, figures, or puppets
  • Storytelling and role-playing
  • Therapeutic games
  • Emotion-identification exercises
  • Calming and coping activities
  • Structured problem-solving

What Is Child-Centered Play Therapy?

Child-centered play therapy allows the child to take a more active role in therapeutic play while the therapist provides a safe, structured, and supportive environment.

Rather than directing every activity, the therapist observes how the child communicates, responds to emotions, approaches problems, and interacts with the materials. Those observations and the therapeutic relationship can help the child build confidence, emotional awareness, regulation, and healthier responses.

Play Therapy Is More Than Playtime

Play therapy is not simply allowing a child to play with toys. Activities are used or observed within a therapeutic relationship and connected to specific treatment goals.

Play therapy may be used by itself or combined with child counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy, family therapy, trauma-informed care, or other approaches.

Does Every Child Receive Play Therapy?

No. Play therapy is used when it is developmentally and clinically appropriate. Some children and teens respond better to conversation-based therapy, structured skill-building, cognitive behavioral therapy, family counseling, or a combination of approaches.

Telehealth Therapy for Kids and Teens

Chrysalis Health provides access to mental health services through secure video sessions using a smartphone, tablet, or computer.

Telehealth can make therapy easier to access by reducing travel, scheduling conflicts, and time away from school or work. Some children also feel more comfortable participating from a familiar environment.

Virtual sessions may use many of the same age-appropriate approaches as in-person therapy, including conversation, therapeutic games, creative activities, coping exercises, and caregiver participation.

Telehealth can be especially helpful for families facing transportation challenges, mobility limitations, demanding schedules, or limited access to nearby mental health providers. It is not the best fit for every child or clinical situation. Appropriateness depends on the child’s needs, safety, ability to participate, technology access, home environment, insurance coverage, and treatment recommendations.

Parents and Caregivers Are Part of Treatment

Parents and caregivers often play an important role in therapy for children and teens. You can provide information your child may not recognize or communicate, help establish treatment goals, reinforce new skills between sessions, and help the therapist understand what is happening outside therapy.

Family involvement can be especially important for younger children and may help strengthen communication, relationships, and overall functioning.

The goal is never to blame the child or caregiver. It is to understand the complete situation and build a consistent support system around your child.

Parent involvement may include:

  • Sharing developmental, medical, and behavioral history
  • Helping identify concerns and treatment goals
  • Participating in selected sessions
  • Learning strategies to use at home
  • Supporting skills practiced between appointments
  • Reporting changes in mood, behavior, sleep, or school performance
  • Participating in family therapy when appropriate

Frequently Asked Questions About Children’s Mental Health Services

Consider requesting a mental health assessment when emotional or behavioral concerns continue for several weeks, cause significant distress, or interfere with your child’s life at home, at school, or with friends. Seek help sooner when symptoms are severe, rapidly worsening, or unsafe.

Mental health therapy can help children experiencing anxiety, depression, trauma, grief, anger, behavioral concerns, family changes, school difficulties, social challenges, and problems managing emotions.

A clinician gathers information about the child’s symptoms, behavior, development, health, family circumstances, school experiences, relationships, strengths, and support system. The assessment helps determine which services and treatment approaches may fit the child.

Children are not expected to communicate like adults. A child therapist may use drawing, games, storytelling, play, structured activities, and other age-appropriate approaches to help the child participate and express thoughts or emotions.

Play therapy uses play and developmentally appropriate activities to help children communicate, express emotions, process experiences, and develop healthier coping and problem-solving skills.

Child-centered play therapy gives the child more freedom to lead therapeutic play while the therapist provides a safe, structured, and supportive environment. The therapist uses the child’s play, choices, and responses to better understand concerns and support emotional development.

Telehealth can work well for many children when the treatment approach, technology, home environment, and level of caregiver involvement are appropriate. It may not be suitable for every child or clinical situation.
